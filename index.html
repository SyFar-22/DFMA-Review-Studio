          Tolerance guide

          Written for someone who has never used tolerances before. Read Start here first, then the tab for the tool you need. Every example has a Try it button that opens the tool with the same numbers filled in, so you can follow along.

          What a tolerance is

          No machine makes a part to exactly the size on the drawing. If you ask for 20 mm you will get 19.98 one time and 20.03 the next. A tolerance tells the maker how far from the drawing size the part may be and still work.

          Example: 20 ±0.1 means any size from 19.9 to 20.1 mm is good. A part at 20.12 is scrap. The total band here is 0.2 mm.

          Why it matters for cost. A small tolerance needs slower machines, extra operations and more inspection. Make a size tight only where two parts touch, slide or seal. Everywhere else, a looser tolerance is cheaper and works just as well.

          Words you will see

          Nominal size
          The size written on the drawing, the target. In 20 ±0.1 it is 20.
          Upper limit, lower limit
          The largest and smallest size allowed. For 20 ±0.1 they are 20.1 and 19.9.
          Deviation
          How far a limit is from the nominal size, with a sign. In 25 +0.2/−0.1 the upper deviation is +0.2 and the lower is −0.1.
          Bilateral tolerance
          Allowed both ways from nominal, for example ±0.1 or +0.2/−0.1.
          Unilateral tolerance
          Allowed one way only, for example 25 −0.10/0 means 24.90 to 25.00.
          Limit dimension
          The two limits written directly, for example 20.00–20.13.
          Tolerance types in CAD
          The Tolerance/Precision box in SOLIDWORKS and similar tools offers: None (the general tolerance applies), Basic (a boxed exact value, its tolerance comes from a GD&T frame), Bilateral (+0.2/−0.1), Symmetric (±0.1), Limit (20.00–20.13), MIN (at least this size, no upper limit) and MAX (at most this size). The Upper and lower limits box reads all of them.
          µm (micrometre)
          One thousandth of a millimetre. 0.021 mm = 21 µm. Fits are listed in µm because the numbers are small.
          General tolerance
          A note in the title block, such as ISO 2768-mK, that gives the tolerance for every size that has none written next to it.
          Fit
          How a shaft sits in a hole: with a gap (clearance), pressed in (interference), or in between (transition).
          IT grade
          A number from ISO 286 that sets how wide a tolerance band is for a size. Smaller number, tighter band. IT7 at 20 mm is 21 µm wide.
          GD&T
          Geometric tolerances: shape, orientation and position (flat, square, round, in the right place), written in a feature control frame.
          Datum
          A reference face, axis or point (A, B, C) that measurements start from, like the corner you measure a room from.
          MMC, LMC
          Maximum and least material condition: the size with the most metal (smallest hole, largest pin) and the least metal (largest hole, smallest pin).
          Stack-up
          Adding up the tolerances of several parts in a row to see how big a gap can get.

          A simple routine for your own part

          1. Mark what touches what.On a print or a sketch, circle every face, hole and pin that touches, slides on or seals against another part. Only these need a tight tolerance.
          2. Pick a fit for every hole and shaft pair.Decide: must it slide, locate accurately, or stay pressed together? Use the Limits and fits tab to get the classes and numbers.
          3. Check every gap that matters with a stack-up.For example a cover over a shaft, or a part that must not hit another. Use the worked example on the stack-up tab as a pattern.
          4. Let the general tolerance cover the rest.Write ISO 2768-mK (or your company's note) in the title block. Use the charts to check the result is loose enough to be cheap and tight enough to work.
          5. Add GD&T where position or shape matters.Bolt hole patterns, bearing seats, sealing faces. Use the frame builder and let it check the rules.
          6. Check the cost.Open DFM review and look at the should-cost estimate. If tight tolerances drive the price, relax the ones that are not at an interface.

          When to use it

          Use the charts for any size on the drawing that has no tolerance written next to it. The general tolerance note in the title block, for example ISO 2768-mK, then decides how far the part may be off. The second half of the page explains and checks GD&T frames, and works out bonus tolerance at MMC.

          Part 1: look up a general tolerance, field by field

          1. General tolerance class.Copy it from the title block. The first letter is for sizes: f fine, m medium, c coarse, v very coarse. The second letter is for shape and orientation: H, K or L, from tight to loose. Most machined parts use mK. If your drawing has no note, ask the designer which class applies; do not guess.
          2. Nominal length, mm.Type the size from the drawing. The tiles show the ± tolerance for that size, and the matching row and column light up in the ISO 2768 tables so you can see where the number comes from.
          3. Nominal angle, °.Only needed for angles. Type the angle from the drawing, for example 90.
          4. Shorter leg length, mm.ISO 2768 sets the angle tolerance by the length of the shorter side of the angle, not by the angle itself. A short leg gets a bigger angle tolerance because a small angle error on a short side moves the end only a little.

          What each result tile means

          TileMeaning
          LinearThe ± tolerance for the length you typed, with the smallest and largest size allowed.
          External radius or chamferTolerance for outside radii and chamfer heights of that size (ISO 2768-1 Table 2).
          AngleThe ± angle tolerance in degrees, set by the shorter leg.
          Flatness or straightnessHow far a face may bow or a line may bend, from the H, K or L letter (ISO 2768-2).
          PerpendicularityHow far a face may lean from square, set by the shorter side.
          Circular run-outHow much a round face may wobble when the part is turned on its axis.

          Worked example: a 46 mm length on an ISO 2768-mK drawing

          1. Class mK, length 46.46 mm sits in the row "over 30 up to 120". Class m gives ±0.3 mm, so any size from 45.7 to 46.3 mm passes.
          2. Compare classes.The same length is ±0.15 in class f, ±0.8 in class c and ±1.5 in class v. Choosing f doubles the precision and the cost of every untoleranced size, so only use it when the whole part needs it.
          3. An angle.A 90° corner with a shorter leg of 20 mm, class m: the "over 10 up to 50" row gives ±0°30′, so 89.5° to 90.5° passes.
          4. Shape.With K, a 46 mm face may be out of flat by 0.2 mm and the circular run-out limit is 0.2 mm.

          Part 2: read and build a feature control frame

          A feature control frame is a row of boxes read left to right like a sentence: what is controlled (symbol), how much (zone and value, with modifiers), and measured from where (datums). Example: | ⌖ | Ø0.2 Ⓜ | A | B | C | reads "the position of this hole must be inside a Ø0.2 mm cylinder, at maximum material condition, measured from datums A, then B, then C".

          The builder, field by field

          FieldWhat to choose
          CharacteristicThe kind of control. Form (flatness, straightness, roundness, cylindricity) never uses datums. Orientation (perpendicularity, parallelism, angularity), location (position) and run-out always do. Profile may or may not.
          ZonePlanes for a flat zone between two parallel planes, Ø cylinder for a round zone around an axis. Holes and pins in position almost always use Ø.
          ToleranceThe total width of the zone in mm, for example 0.2. Ø0.2 means the axis may be up to 0.1 from its true position in any direction.
          Material conditionLeave it at RFS (no symbol) unless you want bonus tolerance. Ⓜ is common on clearance holes for bolts; Ⓛ protects a minimum wall. Ⓢ is only for old ASME 1994 drawings.
          Primary, secondary, tertiary datumThe reference letters in order of importance. The primary is usually the face the part sits on, the secondary an edge or hole that stops it sliding, the tertiary stops it turning.
          Datum modifierⓂ or Ⓛ after a datum letter allows datum shift. Only use it when that datum is a hole or pin, not a flat face.
          Ⓟ projected zoneTick it for threaded or press-fit holes. A box then asks for the projected height: how far above the part the zone extends. Use the thickness of the part the bolt passes through, so a tilted thread cannot push the bolt into the side of the hole in the other part.
          Ⓤ unequal profileOnly for profile tolerances. A box asks how much of the zone lies outside the material. With a profile of 0.4 and 0.1 outside, the surface may be 0.1 proud or 0.3 under the true shape.
          Ⓣ, Ⓕ, ST, CF, CZTangent plane, free state (for flexible parts), statistical tolerance, continuous feature and combined zone. The Modifiers table on the page explains each one.
          ResetPuts the frame back to the starting example: position Ø0.1 Ⓜ | A | B | C.

          Below the frame, every rule is checked. Fail is a real error (for example flatness with a datum, or Ⓜ on a flat face). Review is legal but unusual. Note is extra information. The frame is correct when only Pass and Notes are left.

          Part 3: bonus tolerance at MMC, field by field

          1. Feature.Hole if the material is around it, pin or shaft if it is a solid round.
          2. Size at MMC.The size with the most material: the smallest hole or the largest pin allowed by the size tolerance.
          3. Size at LMC.The size with the least material: the largest hole or the smallest pin.
          4. Actual measured size.What the inspector measured on the real part.
          5. Stated position tolerance Ø.The value in the frame.

          Example: a hole Ø3.6 to Ø3.8 with ⌖ Ø0.2 Ⓜ. MMC is 3.6 and LMC is 3.8. The hole measured 3.7, so it is 0.1 bigger than MMC. That 0.1 is the bonus, and the total position tolerance for this part becomes 0.2 + 0.1 = Ø0.3. At the largest hole, 3.8, it would be Ø0.4. The virtual condition is 3.6 − 0.2 = Ø3.4: a gauge pin of 3.4 mm placed at the true position must go into every good hole.

          Why this is fair: a bigger hole leaves more room around the bolt, so the hole can sit further from its true position and the parts still go together.

          What a stack-up is

          When several parts sit in a row, their size errors add up. Two blocks in a pocket: if the pocket comes out small and both blocks come out big, the blocks may not fit. A tolerance stack-up adds the sizes and their tolerances along a path, called the loop, to find the smallest and largest value of one result, usually a gap. You then compare it with what the design needs.

          The one rule to remember. Walk from one side of the gap, through the parts, to the other side. A size you walk forward along (in the direction you chose as +) adds. A size you walk back along subtracts. The gap is the sum.

          Words used in stack-ups

          Loop
          The path of sizes from one side of the gap to the other. Every size you cross is one row in the calculator.
          Contributor
          One size in the loop, with its tolerance.
          Direction
          + if the size makes the gap bigger when it grows, − if it makes the gap smaller.
          Sensitivity
          How much the gap moves when the size moves 1 mm. It is 1 for sizes in line with the gap, and the cosine of the angle for sizes at an angle.
          Worst case
          Every size at its worst limit at the same time. Safe but pessimistic.
          RSS
          Root sum of squares. Assumes sizes vary at random and rarely all hit their limits together. Gives a smaller, more realistic spread for many parts.
          Distribution
          How sizes spread in production. Normal: most parts near the middle. Uniform: any size in the band is equally likely (tool wear, hand fitting).
          Mean shift
          When the process is centred off the middle of the band. Modified RSS adds a safety factor for it.
          Cpk
          A capability number. 1.33 or more means the gap stays well inside its limits.

          How to do a stack-up, step by step, with the calculator

          1. Write down the requirement.What is the smallest and largest gap that works? Type them into Gap minimum (LSL) and Gap maximum (USL). Leave one empty if there is only one limit.
          2. Sketch the loop.On paper, draw the parts and an arrow for every size from one side of the gap to the other. Pick one direction as +, usually left to right.
          3. Add one row per size.Press Add dimension. Give it a name you will recognise, such as "Pocket width". Remove a row with ×.
          4. Enter the nominal.The size from the drawing, without the tolerance.
          5. Enter + tol and − tol.Both as positive numbers. For 25 −0.10/0 type + tol 0 and − tol 0.1. Leave both blank to use the general tolerance (ISO 2768) from the charts page, or press Fill blank tolerances from ISO 2768 to write those values in.
          6. Set the direction.+ adds if the gap grows when this size grows, − subtracts if the gap shrinks.
          7. Set the sensitivity.Keep 1 for sizes in line with the gap. For a size at angle θ to the gap direction, type cos θ, for example 0.866 for 30°.
          8. Pick the distribution.Normal for machined sizes from a stable process. Uniform for sizes that may sit anywhere in the band. This only changes the Monte Carlo result.
          9. Read the results.The tiles, the loop drawing, the histogram and the contribution bars are explained below.
          10. Fix the biggest contributor.If the gap fails, tighten the size with the longest bar first, or change the design so fewer sizes are in the loop. Fewer contributors is the cheapest fix.

          Worked example: two blocks in a pocket

          A pocket 50 ±0.10 holds block A 24.80 ±0.05 and block B 25.00 −0.10/0. The blocks must slide in, so the gap must be at least 0.05, and must not rattle, so at most 0.40.

          Step 1: make every tolerance equal both ways

          Block B is unilateral. Move its nominal to the middle of the band: mean = 25.00 + (0 + (−0.10)) ÷ 2 = 24.95, tolerance = (0 − (−0.10)) ÷ 2 = ±0.05. So B is 24.95 ±0.05, the same band of 24.90 to 25.00. The calculator does this for you when you type + tol 0 and − tol 0.1.

          Step 2: the nominal gap

          Gap = pocket − A − B = 50 − 24.80 − 24.95 = 0.25 mm

          Step 3: worst case

          Worst case = 0.10 + 0.05 + 0.05 = ±0.20 mm
Gap range  = 0.25 − 0.20 to 0.25 + 0.20 = 0.05 to 0.45 mm

          The smallest gap 0.05 meets the minimum, but the largest gap 0.45 is above 0.40. Worst case says the design can fail.

          Step 4: RSS (statistical)

          RSS = √(0.10² + 0.05² + 0.05²) = √(0.0100 + 0.0025 + 0.0025) = √0.015 = ±0.1225 mm
Gap range = 0.25 ± 0.1225 = 0.1275 to 0.3725 mm

          RSS says almost every assembly is fine, because it is unlikely that all three sizes hit their limits together.

          Step 5: decide and fix

          The pocket gives 0.0100 of the 0.0150 in the RSS sum, which is 67 % of the variation, so it is the size to tighten. With the pocket at 50 ±0.05, worst case becomes ±0.15 and the gap is 0.10 to 0.40, which meets the requirement even at the worst case. Whether you need that depends on volume and risk, see the decision guide below.

          In the calculator, the "Nominal gap" tile shows 0.20 because it uses the sizes as written (block B as 25.00). The worst case and RSS ranges use the real band, so they match the numbers above.

          What every result means

          Which method to use

          Use worst case if

          the part is safety critical, the batch is small, there are only two or three sizes in the loop, or a failure is very expensive. It costs more because tolerances must be tighter.

          Use RSS if

          you build many assemblies, there are four or more sizes, and the processes are stable and centred. A few out-of-limit assemblies must be acceptable or caught by inspection.

          Use modified RSS if

          you want RSS savings but are not sure the processes are centred. It sits between RSS and worst case.

          Use Monte Carlo if

          some sizes are uniform or skewed, the loop has angles, or you need the percentage of bad assemblies.

          Sizes at an angle (2D stacks)

          If a size is not in line with the gap, only part of its change reaches the gap. Multiply by the cosine of the angle between the size and the gap direction and type that as the sensitivity. Example: a 10 ±0.1 mm arm at 30° to the gap has sensitivity cos 30° = 0.866, so its tolerance adds ±0.0866 to the gap, not ±0.1. At 90° the sensitivity is 0 and the size does not affect the gap at all.

          GD&T in a stack

          A position tolerance Øt lets a feature move up to ±t/2 in any direction, so enter it as a row with nominal 0 and ±t/2. Example: a hole with ⌖ Ø0.2 is a row of 0 ±0.1. With Ⓜ, add the bonus the size tolerance allows, because a hole made larger may also sit further off.

          Bolted joints: the fastener formulas

          These formulas tell you how much position tolerance each bolt hole may have so the bolts always fit. Enter the clearance hole at MMC (its smallest allowed diameter, H) and the fastener at MMC (the largest bolt diameter, F).

          • Floating fastener: a bolt and nut through clearance holes in both parts. Each hole may have position tolerance T = H − F.
          • Fixed fastener: one part is threaded, so the bolt is held in place. The gap is shared by both parts: T = (H − F) ÷ 2 each. Add a projected zone Ⓟ to the threaded hole, equal to the thickness of the other part (the optional third field).
          M4 bolt, F = 4.0 mm. Medium clearance hole (ISO 273), H = 4.5 mm.
Floating: T = 4.5 − 4.0         = Ø0.5 per hole  →  ⌖ Ø0.5 Ⓜ | A | B | C
Fixed:    T = (4.5 − 4.0) ÷ 2   = Ø0.25 per hole

          Common mistakes

          • A wrong direction on one size. Check the loop drawing against your sketch.
          • Mixing a unilateral size in without converting it. Type + tol and − tol separately and let the calculator do it.
          • Leaving out a size: a washer, a gasket, a coating or plating thickness, or a datum that is not where you think.
          • Using RSS for two or three sizes or a small batch. Use worst case.
          • Forgetting temperature: a 100 mm aluminium part grows about 0.023 mm for every 10 °C.
          • Tightening every tolerance. Tighten only the biggest contributor; the rest cost money and change little.

          When to use it

          Use limits and fits whenever a round part goes into a round hole: shafts in bearings, pins in holes, bushes in housings, wheels on axles. The fit decides whether the parts slide (clearance), locate accurately (transition) or stay pressed together (interference).

          How a class like H7 or g6 works

          Capital letters are holes, small letters are shafts. The letter sets where the band sits compared with the nominal size. The number is the IT grade, which sets how wide the band is.

          • H hole: the band starts at the nominal size and goes up. H7 at 20 mm is 20.000 to 20.021.
          • h shaft: the band ends at the nominal size and goes down. h6 at 20 mm is 19.987 to 20.000.
          • Shaft letters a to g sit below the nominal: they give clearance in an H hole. js is centred. k to n give transition. p to zc sit above: they give interference.
          • Hole letters work the other way round: A to G sit above the nominal and give clearance on an h shaft, K to N transition, P to ZC interference.

          Hole basis or shaft basis

          Hole basis system (most common)

          Every hole is H, so its smallest size is the nominal size. You choose the fit with the shaft letter: H7/g6 slides, H7/k6 locates, H7/p6 presses. Use it by default: holes are made with standard drills, reamers and plug gauges, while a shaft is easy to turn or grind to any size.

          Shaft basis system

          Every shaft is h, so its largest size is the nominal size. You choose the fit with the hole letter: G7/h6 slides, K7/h6 locates, P7/h6 presses. Use it when one ground shaft or bright bar carries several parts with different fits, or when pins and shafts are bought ready-made in h tolerance.

          Both systems give the same clearances for twin fits. At 20 mm, H7/g6 and G7/h6 both give 0.007 to 0.041 mm clearance. Choose the system with the toggle at the top of the fit panel.

          The calculator, field by field

          1. Hole basis system / Shaft basis system.Choose the system first. It sets the starting classes, the list of preferred fits and the recommendations.
          2. Nominal size, mm.The diameter both parts share, for example 20 for a Ø20 pin in a Ø20 hole. From above 0 up to 500 mm.
          3. Hole letter and hole grade, shaft letter and shaft grade.The two classes of the fit.           5. A negative clearance is interference. The diagram shows both bands against the nominal line.
          6. Use your own limits.When a supplier or an old drawing gives deviations instead of classes, type the nominal size and the four deviations in mm. Above nominal is +, below is −. The fit type and the clearance range are worked out.
          7. Preferred fits at this size.Every preferred fit of your system at your size, as a chart and a table. Click a row to load it.
          8. Upper and lower limits.Type any callout, such as 20 ±0.1, 20 +0.2/-0.1, 20.00-20.13, Ø20 H7 or 20 g6, and read the largest, smallest and mean size. Machine to the mean size.

          Worked example 1: a bearing housing, hole basis

          A ball bearing with a 90 mm outside diameter sits in a stationary housing under normal load. Bearing makers usually give H7 for this housing bore. At Ø90, H7 is +0.035 / 0, so the bore is made between 90.000 and 90.035 mm. Write Ø90 H7 on the drawing. The bearing ring has its own tolerance from the bearing maker, so check their catalogue for the final choice.

          Worked example 2: a sliding bush on a ground shaft, shaft basis

          A Ø20 ground shaft is bought in h6: 19.987 to 20.000 mm. A bush must slide on it, so choose the sliding fit G7/h6. The bush bore G7 is 20.007 to 20.028 mm. The clearance is 20.007 − 20.000 = 0.007 at the tightest and 20.028 − 19.987 = 0.041 mm at the loosest. The shaft stays standard and only the bush is machined to suit.

          Worked example 3: a dowel pin, using your own limits

          A Ø6 dowel pin is made m6: +0.012 / +0.004. It goes into a reamed H7 hole: +0.012 / 0. In Use your own limits, type nominal 6, hole lower 0, hole upper 0.012, shaft lower 0.004, shaft upper 0.012. The result is a transition fit: from 0.012 mm interference (smallest hole, largest pin) to 0.008 mm clearance (largest hole, smallest pin). The pin locates accurately and is tapped in.

          Good habits

          • Start from the function (slide, locate, press), then pick the fit from the table, then check the numbers.
          • Prefer the preferred fits. Tools, gauges and suppliers are ready for them.
          • Keep the hole grade one coarser than the shaft. A precise hole is harder to make than a precise shaft.
          • Plating and anodising add thickness. Machine the size before coating so the coated size is inside the limits.
          • For press fits, check the stress in thin hubs and how much force the press needs.

          Learn DFMA

          Short, practical lessons for new engineers: how to run a DFM and a DFA review, how cost is built up, which machines and materials exist, and how 3D printing and sheet metal differ.

          What DFM is

          Design for Manufacture (DFM) means shaping a part so it can be made with fewer operations, cheaper tools, fewer machine setups and less scrap, without losing its function. About 70 to 80 % of a product's cost is decided while it is being designed, so the cheapest time to fix a manufacturing problem is before the drawing is released.

          A DFM review in 7 steps

          1. Write down your assumptions.Annual volume, target process, material and grade, and which supplier or machine will make it. The right answer changes with these: a part made 10 times a year and 100,000 times a year should be designed differently.
          2. Choose the process by volume, tolerance and cost, not by habit.Low volume and complex: CNC machining or 3D printing. Medium volume: casting or sheet metal. High volume: die casting, injection moulding, stamping. The Configure page lets you switch process and see the findings change.
          3. Run the automatic checks.Upload the STEP file and open DFM review. Each finding shows where the problem is on the model and the solution first.
          4. Go through the checklist.The checklist under the findings lists what software cannot judge, such as whether a tight tolerance is really needed. Mark each line OK or Flag.
          5. Rank the flags by cost.A flag is a candidate, not a verdict. The same tight tolerance can add pennies on one face and a lot on another. The cost estimate shows which cost driver dominates: material, machine time, setups or secondary operations.
          6. Change the design where it pays.Relax tolerances that are not at an interface, open up internal corner radii, reduce setups, use standard holes and threads, and specify finish only on faces that need it.
          7. Review with the supplier and record the decisions.Mark each finding pass, fail or N/A. The tool learns your decisions for the next part.

          General DFM principles

          From the Boothroyd Dewhurst DFM design review checklist
          • Simplify geometry to reduce operations and tool changes.
          • Keep tight tolerances only at interfaces; relax the rest.
          • Specify surface finish only on mating, sealing or cosmetic faces.
          • Use standard stock sizes and common material grades.
          • Make features reachable without special fixtures or awkward setups.
          • Standardise hole sizes, threads and hardware across the design.
          • Select the process on volume, tolerance and cost.
          • Apply DFA too, so part optimisation does not miss assembly savings.

          Process checklists

          Use them as prompts for engineering and supplier review, not as absolute rules

          What DFA is

          Design for Assembly (DFA) makes a product quicker and cheaper to put together, mainly by removing parts and fasteners. Fewer parts means fewer operations, fewer chances of a mistake and lower cost, not just in the factory but in purchasing, stock and service. Published Boothroyd Dewhurst case studies report part counts falling 30 to 60 %; for example one analyser went from 183 parts and 63 fasteners to 31 parts and none.

          A DFA review step by step

          1. Take a baseline.Upload the assembly. The Parts table lists every part with its quantity, and the tiles show part count, estimated assembly time and the DFA index.
          2. Ask the three questions for every part.Does it move relative to the parts it touches? Must it be a different material (insulation, wear, sealing)? Must it be separate so the product can be assembled or serviced? If the answer to all three is no, the part is a candidate to combine with a neighbour or remove.
          3. Attack fasteners first.A screw costs far more to drive than to buy. Replace screws with snap fits, tabs, press fits or fewer, larger fasteners. Standardise on one screw type.
          4. Make each remaining part easy to handle.Avoid parts that are tiny, sharp, flexible, slippery or that tangle and nest. Make parts symmetric so orientation does not matter, or clearly asymmetric so they only fit one way.
          5. Make each part easy to insert.Add chamfers and lead-ins, self-locating features (pins, slots, tabs), assembly from one direction (top-down), and no need to hold a part while fixing it. Add mistake-proofing so a part cannot be fitted wrongly.
          6. Compare and validate.Compare the new design with the baseline. Check strength, tolerances, service and regulations before you accept a merged part.

          The DFA index

          DFA index = (3 s × theoretical minimum number of parts) ÷ total estimated assembly time. Three seconds is the ideal time to handle and insert one part. An index of 5 to 10 % is common for an unoptimised design; good designs reach 25 % and more. The Assembly DFA page calculates it from the part types you set.

          Watch the whole manufacturing system

          A DFA estimate adds operations one after another. On a real line an operator loads a machine, starts it, and does other work while it runs, so ultrasonic welding, heat staking, testing and inspection cycles overlap with manual work. A study at Kohler (Moseley) found the plain DFA total was too high until machine cycle times and parallel work were laid out in a standard work combination table. When your product uses such machines, add their cycle times and check what the operator does during them.

          Design for modularity

          Modularity cuts cost above the single product. Bottom-up: choose preferred parts (fasteners, fittings, terminals) so fewer part numbers are bought, stocked and tracked. Top-down: define standard interfaces between sub-assemblies so one module fits many product lines; control each interface with an interface specification drawing and change it only after review. The Raymond Corporation reduced twelve series of load backrest weldments to three modular series this way.

          What should-cost is

          Should-cost is what a part ought to cost to make, built up from its material, process and volume, not copied from a quote. You compare it with the supplier's price and talk about the specific drivers (cycle time, setups, yield, tooling) instead of the headline number.

          Should-cost = material + processing + setup + tooling + scrap + overhead + profit

          Build it in 8 steps

          1. Define the scope.Annual volume, batch size, region, delivery terms.
          2. Material.Buy weight (stock, not finished weight) × price per kg, plus handling.
          3. List every operation.Primary process, secondary operations, finishing, inspection.
          4. Time each operation.Cycle time and setup time from the geometry and process.
          5. Apply rates.Machine and labour rate per hour for the right machine class and region. If the machine rate already includes the operator, do not add labour again.
          6. Spread one-off costs.Setup over the batch, tooling over its life in parts.
          7. Add scrap, overhead and profit.Divide by good parts, not parts started.
          8. Compare with the quote.Find the biggest difference and question that assumption first.

          What to change when one cost dominates

          Material dominates

          Near-net shapes (casting, forging, extrusion), standard stock sizes, nesting, a cheaper grade.

          Machine time dominates

          Simpler geometry, larger radii, fewer setups, relaxed tolerances, a better suited process.

          Setups dominate

          Bigger batches, features reachable from fewer sides, standard fixtures.

          Tooling dominates

          Standard tools, merged features, higher volume per tool, or a process without dedicated tooling.

          Secondary operations dominate

          Finish and coating only where needed, localised tight tolerances, fewer inspection points.

          Volume changes everything

          Setup and tooling are paid once per batch or per tool. At 10 parts they can be most of the price; at 10,000 parts they almost disappear. That is why the cost estimate in DFM review shows several quantities side by side. The same logic decides between processes: the deep drawing tutorial from Boothroyd Dewhurst shows a drawn box costing more than the same box stamped and bent, with about a quarter of the tooling investment for the stamped version, but with open corners. Combining operations in one compound tool raised tooling cost and lowered the part price. Always compare at your real volume.

          The seven families of 3D printing

          ISO/ASTM 52900
          Material extrusion

          A nozzle lays down melted plastic (FDM, FFF) or metal-filled filament. Cheap and common; visible layers; weakest between layers.

          Vat photopolymerisation

          Light hardens liquid resin layer by layer (SLA laser, DLP projector, MSLA screen, Carbon DLS). Fine detail and smooth surfaces.

          Powder bed fusion

          A laser or electron beam melts powder (SLS and MJF for nylon; DMLS, SLM, EBM for metal). Strong parts; polymer needs no supports, metal does.

          Material jetting

          Print heads jet droplets of resin and cure them (PolyJet). Full colour and multi-material prototypes.

          Binder jetting

          Glue is printed into powder; metal parts are then sintered and shrink about 20 %. Fast for many small metal parts, also used for sand casting moulds.

          Directed energy deposition

          Wire or powder is melted as it is deposited (DED, LMD, WAAM). Large near-net metal parts and repairs, always machined after.

          Sheet lamination

          Sheets are bonded and cut (LOM, ultrasonic). Rare today.

          Technologies and their design limits

          Design rules for 3D printing

          1. Respect the minimum wall and hole.Below the minimum, walls break or do not print and small holes close up. The table above lists them per technology.
          2. Orient for strength and finish.FDM parts are weakest between layers: put loads along the layers. Faces facing down onto supports are rougher.
          3. Avoid unsupported overhangs.FDM, resin and metal powder need supports past about 45 to 60°; supports leave marks and add cost. Nylon powder and MJF need none.
          4. Let powder and resin out.Hollow parts need two escape holes (at least Ø3 to 5 mm) so trapped powder or resin can be removed.
          5. Plan finishing for tight features.Bearing seats, threads and sealing faces are usually drilled, reamed, tapped or machined after printing.
          6. Use the freedom.Merge parts, add internal channels and lattices, and remove fasteners. That is where printing beats machining on cost.

          How a drilled hole is made

          Read this before you model holes and threads

          A hole is not cut by an end mill like a pocket. It is made by a twist drill that leaves its own shape: a round wall and a cone at the bottom from the drill point. A hole that is drawn like that in CAD is normal and costs nothing extra, so the DFM review does not report the cone or the edges inside a hole as sharp corners.

          1. Spot or centre drill.A short stiff drill makes a small cone so the twist drill starts in the right place and does not wander. Always used on CNC.
          2. Pilot drill (for big holes).Holes above about Ø12 mm are often started with a smaller pilot drill, about one third of the final size and a little larger than the centre web of the big drill. It cuts the force on the big drill and keeps it straight.
          3. Twist drill to size.A 118° point is the general purpose drill; 135° is used for hard steel and stainless. The point adds about 0.3 × the drill diameter below the full-size depth. Up to 4×D deep is standard, up to about 10×D needs peck drilling (the drill backs out to clear chips), deeper needs gun drilling.
          4. Ream for accuracy (optional).A drill gives about IT10 to IT11. For a fit such as H7, drill slightly undersize and ream: leave about 0.1 to 0.2 mm on the diameter up to Ø5, 0.2 to 0.3 mm from Ø5 to Ø20, and 0.3 to 0.4 mm above. The reamer improves size, roundness, straightness and surface finish.
          5. Chamfer or countersink the entry.A small chamfer removes the burr and guides the tap or the bolt. A 90° countersink takes a flat head screw.
          Through hole

          Goes all the way. Easiest to make, chips fall out, a tap can run right through. Write THRU.

          Blind hole

          Stops inside the part and ends in the drill cone. Give the depth of the full diameter, for example Ø8 ↧20. A flat bottom needs an extra end mill or a flat drill, so only ask for it when it matters.

          Tapping: making a thread in a hole

          A tapped hole is a drilled hole with an inside thread cut or formed by a tool called a tap. A threaded hole is the wider name for any hole with a thread, however it is made: tapping, thread milling, or a threaded insert. See also UltiRapid: tapping vs threaded holes.

          1. Drill the tap drill hole.The hole is smaller than the thread size, so the tap has metal to cut the thread into. For an ISO metric coarse thread cut by a tap, tap drill ≈ thread size − pitch. M6×1 → Ø5.0, M8×1.25 → Ø6.8, M20×2.5 → Ø17.5. The table below lists them all.
          2. Chamfer the entry.A 90° chamfer slightly larger than the thread size helps the tap start straight and stops a raised burr.
          3. Choose the tap.Taper tap (long lead) starts easily, used by hand. Plug tap is the general one. Bottoming tap (short lead) cuts thread close to the bottom of a blind hole. Spiral point taps push chips forward, best for through holes. Spiral flute taps pull chips back out, best for blind holes. Forming (roll) taps press the thread without chips; they need a larger drill, about thread size − half the pitch, and suit aluminium, copper and soft steel.
          4. Tap by machine or by hand.On a CNC machine the spindle feeds exactly one pitch per turn (rigid tapping). By hand, turn forward and back to break the chips.
          5. Other ways to get a thread.Thread milling cuts the thread with a small rotating cutter: any size with one tool, no broken tap risk, good for hard or expensive parts. Threaded inserts (helical wire inserts, or heat-set and press-in inserts in plastic) give a strong, wear-resistant thread in soft material.

          How deep: thread depth and drill depth

          1. Thread depth from the material.The bolt needs enough engaged thread so the bolt breaks before the thread strips: about 1 × D in steel, 1.5 × D in cast iron, 2 × D in aluminium and 2.5 × D in plastics. More than about 3 × D adds no strength.
          2. Add room for the tap lead.The first threads of a tap are not full size. Drill at least 3 to 5 pitches deeper than the full thread for a plug tap, or about 1.5 to 2 pitches for a bottoming tap.
          3. The drill point comes on top.The cone below the full-diameter depth is about 0.3 × the drill diameter. Make sure it does not break into another hole or come out of the far side.

Tap drill            = 20 − 2.5            = Ø17.5
Thread depth         = 40 mm (at least 1 × D = 20; 40 gives margin)
Tap lead allowance   = 4 pitches × 2.5     = 10 mm

          In the 3D model, cosmetic threads are fine. Model the hole at the tap drill size and the drilled depth, so the DFM review recognises it as a tapped hole and checks its engagement.

          Hole sizes for ISO metric coarse threads

          Values in mm. Check a tap or insert maker's chart for special materials.

          Tap drill (cutting tap) and clearance holes follow ISO 2306 and ISO 273 practice. Forming tap drills are about thread size − 0.5 × pitch. Helical insert drills are the usual values for wire thread inserts. Counterbores suit ISO 4762 socket head cap screws (DIN 974-1); make the counterbore about 0.5 mm deeper than the head height (head height = thread size).

          Design rules for holes and threads

          • Use standard drill sizes and standard threads, and as few different sizes as possible on one part: every size is another tool change.
          • Keep blind tapped holes from needing thread all the way to the bottom; leave room for the tap lead.
          • Avoid taps smaller than M3 in hard steel or stainless; small taps break easily. Use M3 or larger, or thread milling.
          • Keep holes 4×D deep or less where possible; deeper holes need peck drilling or gun drilling.
          • Start and end holes on a flat face square to the drill. On a curved or sloping face, add a spot face first.
          • Keep holes at least one diameter from an edge and from other holes so the wall does not bulge or break.
          • For soft or plastic parts that are screwed many times, use threaded inserts.
          • For a press or slide fit, ream the hole and give it an ISO 286 class such as H7.

          Sheet metal rules

          From the Boothroyd Dewhurst checklist
          • Bend radius at least the material thickness.
          • Hole to edge at least 2 × thickness.
          • Hole to bend at least 2 × thickness + bend radius.
          • Use a standard sheet thickness (gauge).
          • Nest the flat pattern efficiently on standard sheet.
          • Check the bend sequence can be done without the part hitting the tool.
          • Add bend reliefs, tabs and slots where needed.
          • Meet the minimum flange length for every bend.
          • Keep hardware insertion points reachable after forming.

          Deep drawing or stamp and bend?

          Cup and box shapes can be deep drawn from one blank in a series of draws, restrikes, trims and punches, or cut flat and bent on a press brake. Drawing gives closed, rounded corners and suits high volume; it needs more dedicated tooling. Stamping and bending needs much less tooling and is often cheaper per part at moderate volume, but leaves open corners and may move features such as flange holes. Combining operations in one compound tool (for example trimming and punching together) costs more tooling but saves processing per part. Compare both routes at your real volume before choosing.

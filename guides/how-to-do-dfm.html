How to do a DFM review in 7 steps

A practical design for manufacture review: process choice, automatic checks, checklists, cost ranking and supplier review.

What DFM is

Design for Manufacture (DFM) means shaping a part so it can be made with fewer operations, cheaper tools, fewer machine setups and less scrap, without losing its function. About 70 to 80 % of a product's cost is decided while it is being designed, so the cheapest time to fix a manufacturing problem is before the drawing is released.

A DFM review in 7 steps

  1. Write down your assumptions.Annual volume, target process, material and grade, and which supplier or machine will make it. The right answer changes with these: a part made 10 times a year and 100,000 times a year should be designed differently.
  2. Choose the process by volume, tolerance and cost, not by habit.Low volume and complex: CNC machining or 3D printing. Medium volume: casting or sheet metal. High volume: die casting, injection moulding, stamping. The Configure page lets you switch process and see the findings change.
  3. Run the automatic checks.Upload the STEP file and open DFM review. Each finding shows where the problem is on the model and the solution first.
  4. Go through the checklist.The checklist under the findings lists what software cannot judge, such as whether a tight tolerance is really needed. Mark each line OK or Flag.
  5. Rank the flags by cost.A flag is a candidate, not a verdict. The same tight tolerance can add pennies on one face and a lot on another. The cost estimate shows which cost driver dominates: material, machine time, setups or secondary operations.
  6. Change the design where it pays.Relax tolerances that are not at an interface, open up internal corner radii, reduce setups, use standard holes and threads, and specify finish only on faces that need it.
  7. Review with the supplier and record the decisions.Mark each finding pass, fail or N/A. The tool learns your decisions for the next part.

General DFM principles

From the Boothroyd Dewhurst DFM design review checklist
  • Simplify geometry to reduce operations and tool changes.
  • Keep tight tolerances only at interfaces; relax the rest.
  • Specify surface finish only on mating, sealing or cosmetic faces.
  • Use standard stock sizes and common material grades.
  • Make features reachable without special fixtures or awkward setups.
  • Standardise hole sizes, threads and hardware across the design.
  • Select the process on volume, tolerance and cost.
  • Apply DFA too, so part optimisation does not miss assembly savings.

Process checklists

Use them as prompts for engineering and supplier review, not as absolute rules
CNC machining
  • Critical features reachable with the fewest practical setups
  • Internal corner radii large enough for standard end mills
  • Pocket depth-to-width ratio within tool capability (under 4:1)
  • Tight tolerances limited to functional interfaces only
  • Part can be fixtured without special tooling
  • Finish requirements specified only where needed
  • Material grade readily available in standard stock forms
  • EDM, wire EDM or hand finishing avoided unless justified
  • Thread sizes and depths standardised across the design
Injection moulding
  • Wall thickness as uniform as practical for resin, flow and geometry
  • Draft reviewed for material, texture, wall depth and ejection
  • Undercuts eliminated or manageable with simple side-actions
  • Ribs no more than 60 % of the adjacent wall to prevent sink
  • Gate locations chosen to minimise flow length and weld lines
  • Part ejects cleanly without sticking or distortion
  • Snap fits and living hinges within material limits
  • Boss diameters and walls follow resin guidelines
  • No sharp internal corners (stress raisers)
Sheet metal
  • Bend radii at least the material thickness
  • Hole-to-edge spacing at least 2 × thickness
  • Hole-to-bend spacing at least 2 × thickness + bend radius
  • Gauge is a standard available thickness
  • Flat pattern nests efficiently on standard sheet
  • Bend sequence feasible without interference
  • Tabs, slots and relief cuts included where needed
  • Minimum flange length met for all bends
  • Hardware insertion locations accessible after forming
Die casting
  • Wall sections reasonably uniform
  • Draft on die-pull surfaces per alloy, depth and supplier guidance
  • Parting line and ejector locations acceptable
  • Cores can be pulled without complex slides
  • As-cast tolerances acceptable, or secondary machining planned
  • Porosity risk reviewed for structural, sealing and machined areas
  • Alloy compatible with die life at the required volume
  • No deep thin ribs that increase thermal stress
  • Fillets and radii adequate for metal flow
Investment casting
  • Wall thickness achievable for the selected alloy
  • No re-entrant features that prevent wax extraction
  • Gate and riser locations planned for clean fill
  • Tolerances achievable as cast, or machining planned
  • Surface finish requirements compatible with the process
  • Part volume and weight within casting capability
  • Core complexity manageable with soluble or ceramic cores
  • Inspection requirements defined for internal features
Hot forging
  • Geometry achievable in closed-die forging
  • Draft angles adequate for die removal (typically 3 to 7°)
  • Fillet radii generous enough for material flow
  • Parting line placed to minimise flash removal
  • Web thickness to rib height ratios within limits
  • Grain flow aligned with the main loads
  • Flash and trimming allowances accounted for
  • Secondary machining only where needed
Extrusion
  • Profile cross-section within press capability
  • Wall thicknesses uniform or within process limits
  • No re-entrant features that prevent die flow
  • Hollow sections achievable with standard mandrels
  • Tolerances achievable as extruded, or machining planned
  • Standard alloy or resin selected for availability
  • Cut lengths and tolerances specified
  • Secondary operations (punch, drill, bend) minimised
PCB fabrication and assembly
  • Component placement accessible for pick-and-place
  • Pad geometries follow IPC standards for the process
  • Trace widths and spacing within fab house capability
  • Via sizes and types suit the layer count
  • Thermal relief pads on ground and power planes
  • Test points accessible for ICT or flying probe
  • Solder paste stencil openings suit the component pitch
  • Fiducial marks present for alignment
3D printing
  • Walls and holes above the minimum for the chosen technology
  • Orientation chosen for strength (loads along the layers) and finish
  • Overhangs self-supporting or supports acceptable on those faces
  • Escape holes for trapped powder or resin in hollow sections
  • Tight fits, threads and sealing faces planned for post-machining
  • Parts merged and fasteners removed where printing allows
  • Build size within the machine envelope, with room for nesting
  • Material and post-processing (heat treatment, support removal) agreed with the supplier

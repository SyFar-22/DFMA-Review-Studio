Drilling and tapping: tap drill sizes, thread depth and clearance holes

How drilled and tapped holes are made, how deep to drill, and a full table of tap drill, insert and clearance hole sizes.

How a drilled hole is made

Read this before you model holes and threads

A hole is not cut by an end mill like a pocket. It is made by a twist drill that leaves its own shape: a round wall and a cone at the bottom from the drill point. A hole that is drawn like that in CAD is normal and costs nothing extra, so the DFM review does not report the cone or the edges inside a hole as sharp corners.

  1. Spot or centre drill.A short stiff drill makes a small cone so the twist drill starts in the right place and does not wander. Always used on CNC.
  2. Pilot drill (for big holes).Holes above about Ø12 mm are often started with a smaller pilot drill, about one third of the final size and a little larger than the centre web of the big drill. It cuts the force on the big drill and keeps it straight.
  3. Twist drill to size.A 118° point is the general purpose drill; 135° is used for hard steel and stainless. The point adds about 0.3 × the drill diameter below the full-size depth. Up to 4×D deep is standard, up to about 10×D needs peck drilling (the drill backs out to clear chips), deeper needs gun drilling.
  4. Ream for accuracy (optional).A drill gives about IT10 to IT11. For a fit such as H7, drill slightly undersize and ream: leave about 0.1 to 0.2 mm on the diameter up to Ø5, 0.2 to 0.3 mm from Ø5 to Ø20, and 0.3 to 0.4 mm above. The reamer improves size, roundness, straightness and surface finish.
  5. Chamfer or countersink the entry.A small chamfer removes the burr and guides the tap or the bolt. A 90° countersink takes a flat head screw.
Through hole

Goes all the way. Easiest to make, chips fall out, a tap can run right through. Write THRU.

Blind hole

Stops inside the part and ends in the drill cone. Give the depth of the full diameter, for example Ø8 ↧20. A flat bottom needs an extra end mill or a flat drill, so only ask for it when it matters.

Tapping: making a thread in a hole

A tapped hole is a drilled hole with an inside thread cut or formed by a tool called a tap. A threaded hole is the wider name for any hole with a thread, however it is made: tapping, thread milling, or a threaded insert. See also UltiRapid: tapping vs threaded holes.

  1. Drill the tap drill hole.The hole is smaller than the thread size, so the tap has metal to cut the thread into. For an ISO metric coarse thread cut by a tap, tap drill ≈ thread size − pitch. M6×1 → Ø5.0, M8×1.25 → Ø6.8, M20×2.5 → Ø17.5. The table below lists them all.
  2. Chamfer the entry.A 90° chamfer slightly larger than the thread size helps the tap start straight and stops a raised burr.
  3. Choose the tap.Taper tap (long lead) starts easily, used by hand. Plug tap is the general one. Bottoming tap (short lead) cuts thread close to the bottom of a blind hole. Spiral point taps push chips forward, best for through holes. Spiral flute taps pull chips back out, best for blind holes. Forming (roll) taps press the thread without chips; they need a larger drill, about thread size − half the pitch, and suit aluminium, copper and soft steel.
  4. Tap by machine or by hand.On a CNC machine the spindle feeds exactly one pitch per turn (rigid tapping). By hand, turn forward and back to break the chips.
  5. Other ways to get a thread.Thread milling cuts the thread with a small rotating cutter: any size with one tool, no broken tap risk, good for hard or expensive parts. Threaded inserts (helical wire inserts, or heat-set and press-in inserts in plastic) give a strong, wear-resistant thread in soft material.

How deep: thread depth and drill depth

  1. Thread depth from the material.The bolt needs enough engaged thread so the bolt breaks before the thread strips: about 1 × D in steel, 1.5 × D in cast iron, 2 × D in aluminium and 2.5 × D in plastics. More than about 3 × D adds no strength.
  2. Add room for the tap lead.The first threads of a tap are not full size. Drill at least 3 to 5 pitches deeper than the full thread for a plug tap, or about 1.5 to 2 pitches for a bottoming tap.
  3. The drill point comes on top.The cone below the full-diameter depth is about 0.3 × the drill diameter. Make sure it does not break into another hole or come out of the far side.
Example: M20 × 2.5 blind in steel
Tap drill            = 20 − 2.5            = Ø17.5
Thread depth         = 40 mm (at least 1 × D = 20; 40 gives margin)
Tap lead allowance   = 4 pitches × 2.5     = 10 mm
Drill depth          = 40 + 10             = 50 mm (full diameter)
Drill point below it ≈ 0.3 × 17.5          ≈ 5.3 mm

On the drawing:  M20×2.5 ↧40   Drill Ø17.5 ↧50
Through version: M20×2.5 THRU

In the 3D model, cosmetic threads are fine. Model the hole at the tap drill size and the drilled depth, so the DFM review recognises it as a tapped hole and checks its engagement.

Tap drill (cutting tap) and clearance holes follow ISO 2306 and ISO 273 practice. Forming tap drills are about thread size − 0.5 × pitch. Helical insert drills are the usual values for wire thread inserts. Counterbores suit ISO 4762 socket head cap screws (DIN 974-1); make the counterbore about 0.5 mm deeper than the head height (head height = thread size).

Design rules for holes and threads

  • Use standard drill sizes and standard threads, and as few different sizes as possible on one part: every size is another tool change.
  • Keep blind tapped holes from needing thread all the way to the bottom; leave room for the tap lead.
  • Avoid taps smaller than M3 in hard steel or stainless; small taps break easily. Use M3 or larger, or thread milling.
  • Keep holes 4×D deep or less where possible; deeper holes need peck drilling or gun drilling.
  • Start and end holes on a flat face square to the drill. On a curved or sloping face, add a spot face first.
  • Keep holes at least one diameter from an edge and from other holes so the wall does not bulge or break.
  • For soft or plastic parts that are screwed many times, use threaded inserts.
  • For a press or slide fit, ream the hole and give it an ISO 286 class such as H7.

How to do a DFA review (design for assembly)

The three questions, fastener reduction, handling and insertion rules, the DFA index and design for modularity.

What DFA is

Design for Assembly (DFA) makes a product quicker and cheaper to put together, mainly by removing parts and fasteners. Fewer parts means fewer operations, fewer chances of a mistake and lower cost, not just in the factory but in purchasing, stock and service. Published Boothroyd Dewhurst case studies report part counts falling 30 to 60 %; for example one analyser went from 183 parts and 63 fasteners to 31 parts and none.

A DFA review step by step

  1. Take a baseline.Upload the assembly. The Parts table lists every part with its quantity, and the tiles show part count, estimated assembly time and the DFA index.
  2. Ask the three questions for every part.Does it move relative to the parts it touches? Must it be a different material (insulation, wear, sealing)? Must it be separate so the product can be assembled or serviced? If the answer to all three is no, the part is a candidate to combine with a neighbour or remove.
  3. Attack fasteners first.A screw costs far more to drive than to buy. Replace screws with snap fits, tabs, press fits or fewer, larger fasteners. Standardise on one screw type.
  4. Make each remaining part easy to handle.Avoid parts that are tiny, sharp, flexible, slippery or that tangle and nest. Make parts symmetric so orientation does not matter, or clearly asymmetric so they only fit one way.
  5. Make each part easy to insert.Add chamfers and lead-ins, self-locating features (pins, slots, tabs), assembly from one direction (top-down), and no need to hold a part while fixing it. Add mistake-proofing so a part cannot be fitted wrongly.
  6. Compare and validate.Compare the new design with the baseline. Check strength, tolerances, service and regulations before you accept a merged part.

The DFA index

DFA index = (3 s × theoretical minimum number of parts) ÷ total estimated assembly time. Three seconds is the ideal time to handle and insert one part. An index of 5 to 10 % is common for an unoptimised design; good designs reach 25 % and more. The Assembly DFA page calculates it from the part types you set.

Watch the whole manufacturing system

A DFA estimate adds operations one after another. On a real line an operator loads a machine, starts it, and does other work while it runs, so ultrasonic welding, heat staking, testing and inspection cycles overlap with manual work. A study at Kohler (Moseley) found the plain DFA total was too high until machine cycle times and parallel work were laid out in a standard work combination table. When your product uses such machines, add their cycle times and check what the operator does during them.

Design for modularity

Modularity cuts cost above the single product. Bottom-up: choose preferred parts (fasteners, fittings, terminals) so fewer part numbers are bought, stocked and tracked. Top-down: define standard interfaces between sub-assemblies so one module fits many product lines; control each interface with an interface specification drawing and change it only after review. The Raymond Corporation reduced twelve series of load backrest weldments to three modular series this way.

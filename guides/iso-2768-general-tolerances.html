ISO 2768 general tolerances: tables and how to use them

ISO 2768-1 and 2768-2 tables for linear, angular, flatness, perpendicularity and run-out tolerances, with worked examples.

When to use it

Use the charts for any size on the drawing that has no tolerance written next to it. The general tolerance note in the title block, for example ISO 2768-mK, then decides how far the part may be off. The second half of the page explains and checks GD&T frames, and works out bonus tolerance at MMC.

Part 1: look up a general tolerance, field by field

  1. General tolerance class.Copy it from the title block. The first letter is for sizes: f fine, m medium, c coarse, v very coarse. The second letter is for shape and orientation: H, K or L, from tight to loose. Most machined parts use mK. If your drawing has no note, ask the designer which class applies; do not guess.
  2. Nominal length, mm.Type the size from the drawing. The tiles show the ± tolerance for that size, and the matching row and column light up in the ISO 2768 tables so you can see where the number comes from.
  3. Nominal angle, °.Only needed for angles. Type the angle from the drawing, for example 90.
  4. Shorter leg length, mm.ISO 2768 sets the angle tolerance by the length of the shorter side of the angle, not by the angle itself. A short leg gets a bigger angle tolerance because a small angle error on a short side moves the end only a little.

What each result tile means

TileMeaning
LinearThe ± tolerance for the length you typed, with the smallest and largest size allowed.
External radius or chamferTolerance for outside radii and chamfer heights of that size (ISO 2768-1 Table 2).
AngleThe ± angle tolerance in degrees, set by the shorter leg.
Flatness or straightnessHow far a face may bow or a line may bend, from the H, K or L letter (ISO 2768-2).
PerpendicularityHow far a face may lean from square, set by the shorter side.
Circular run-outHow much a round face may wobble when the part is turned on its axis.

Worked example: a 46 mm length on an ISO 2768-mK drawing

Open the calculator
  1. Class mK, length 46.46 mm sits in the row "over 30 up to 120". Class m gives ±0.3 mm, so any size from 45.7 to 46.3 mm passes.
  2. Compare classes.The same length is ±0.15 in class f, ±0.8 in class c and ±1.5 in class v. Choosing f doubles the precision and the cost of every untoleranced size, so only use it when the whole part needs it.
  3. An angle.A 90° corner with a shorter leg of 20 mm, class m: the "over 10 up to 50" row gives ±0°30′, so 89.5° to 90.5° passes.
  4. Shape.With K, a 46 mm face may be out of flat by 0.2 mm and the circular run-out limit is 0.2 mm.

Part 2: read and build a feature control frame

A feature control frame is a row of boxes read left to right like a sentence: what is controlled (symbol), how much (zone and value, with modifiers), and measured from where (datums). Example: | ⌖ | Ø0.2 Ⓜ | A | B | C | reads "the position of this hole must be inside a Ø0.2 mm cylinder, at maximum material condition, measured from datums A, then B, then C".

The builder, field by field

FieldWhat to choose
CharacteristicThe kind of control. Form (flatness, straightness, roundness, cylindricity) never uses datums. Orientation (perpendicularity, parallelism, angularity), location (position) and run-out always do. Profile may or may not.
ZonePlanes for a flat zone between two parallel planes, Ø cylinder for a round zone around an axis. Holes and pins in position almost always use Ø.
ToleranceThe total width of the zone in mm, for example 0.2. Ø0.2 means the axis may be up to 0.1 from its true position in any direction.
Material conditionLeave it at RFS (no symbol) unless you want bonus tolerance. Ⓜ is common on clearance holes for bolts; Ⓛ protects a minimum wall. Ⓢ is only for old ASME 1994 drawings.
Primary, secondary, tertiary datumThe reference letters in order of importance. The primary is usually the face the part sits on, the secondary an edge or hole that stops it sliding, the tertiary stops it turning.
Datum modifierⓂ or Ⓛ after a datum letter allows datum shift. Only use it when that datum is a hole or pin, not a flat face.
Ⓟ projected zoneTick it for threaded or press-fit holes. A box then asks for the projected height: how far above the part the zone extends. Use the thickness of the part the bolt passes through, so a tilted thread cannot push the bolt into the side of the hole in the other part.
Ⓤ unequal profileOnly for profile tolerances. A box asks how much of the zone lies outside the material. With a profile of 0.4 and 0.1 outside, the surface may be 0.1 proud or 0.3 under the true shape.
Ⓣ, Ⓕ, ST, CF, CZTangent plane, free state (for flexible parts), statistical tolerance, continuous feature and combined zone. The Modifiers table on the page explains each one.
ResetPuts the frame back to the starting example: position Ø0.1 Ⓜ | A | B | C.

Below the frame, every rule is checked. Fail is a real error (for example flatness with a datum, or Ⓜ on a flat face). Review is legal but unusual. Note is extra information. The frame is correct when only Pass and Notes are left.

Part 3: bonus tolerance at MMC, field by field

Open the calculator
  1. Feature.Hole if the material is around it, pin or shaft if it is a solid round.
  2. Size at MMC.The size with the most material: the smallest hole or the largest pin allowed by the size tolerance.
  3. Size at LMC.The size with the least material: the largest hole or the smallest pin.
  4. Actual measured size.What the inspector measured on the real part.
  5. Stated position tolerance Ø.The value in the frame.

Example: a hole Ø3.6 to Ø3.8 with ⌖ Ø0.2 Ⓜ. MMC is 3.6 and LMC is 3.8. The hole measured 3.7, so it is 0.1 bigger than MMC. That 0.1 is the bonus, and the total position tolerance for this part becomes 0.2 + 0.1 = Ø0.3. At the largest hole, 3.8, it would be Ø0.4. The virtual condition is 3.6 − 0.2 = Ø3.4: a gauge pin of 3.4 mm placed at the true position must go into every good hole.

Why this is fair: a bigger hole leaves more room around the bolt, so the hole can sit further from its true position and the parts still go together.

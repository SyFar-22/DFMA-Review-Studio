Engineering tolerances explained for beginners

What a tolerance is, how to read any dimension, and which tolerance tool answers which question. Written for new engineers.

What a tolerance is

No machine makes a part to exactly the size on the drawing. If you ask for 20 mm you will get 19.98 one time and 20.03 the next. A tolerance tells the maker how far from the drawing size the part may be and still work.

Example: 20 ±0.1 means any size from 19.9 to 20.1 mm is good. A part at 20.12 is scrap. The total band here is 0.2 mm.

Why it matters for cost. A small tolerance needs slower machines, extra operations and more inspection. Make a size tight only where two parts touch, slide or seal. Everywhere else, a looser tolerance is cheaper and works just as well.

Words you will see

Nominal size
The size written on the drawing, the target. In 20 ±0.1 it is 20.
Upper limit, lower limit
The largest and smallest size allowed. For 20 ±0.1 they are 20.1 and 19.9.
Deviation
How far a limit is from the nominal size, with a sign. In 25 +0.2/−0.1 the upper deviation is +0.2 and the lower is −0.1.
Bilateral tolerance
Allowed both ways from nominal, for example ±0.1 or +0.2/−0.1.
Unilateral tolerance
Allowed one way only, for example 25 −0.10/0 means 24.90 to 25.00.
Limit dimension
The two limits written directly, for example 20.00–20.13.
Tolerance types in CAD
The Tolerance/Precision box in SOLIDWORKS and similar tools offers: None (the general tolerance applies), Basic (a boxed exact value, its tolerance comes from a GD&T frame), Bilateral (+0.2/−0.1), Symmetric (±0.1), Limit (20.00–20.13), MIN (at least this size, no upper limit) and MAX (at most this size). The Upper and lower limits box reads all of them.
µm (micrometre)
One thousandth of a millimetre. 0.021 mm = 21 µm. Fits are listed in µm because the numbers are small.
General tolerance
A note in the title block, such as ISO 2768-mK, that gives the tolerance for every size that has none written next to it.
Fit
How a shaft sits in a hole: with a gap (clearance), pressed in (interference), or in between (transition).
IT grade
A number from ISO 286 that sets how wide a tolerance band is for a size. Smaller number, tighter band. IT7 at 20 mm is 21 µm wide.
GD&T
Geometric tolerances: shape, orientation and position (flat, square, round, in the right place), written in a feature control frame.
Datum
A reference face, axis or point (A, B, C) that measurements start from, like the corner you measure a room from.
MMC, LMC
Maximum and least material condition: the size with the most metal (smallest hole, largest pin) and the least metal (largest hole, smallest pin).
Stack-up
Adding up the tolerances of several parts in a row to see how big a gap can get.

Read any dimension in three steps

  1. Find the nominal size.The main number, for example 25.
  2. Find the tolerance.Next to the number (±0.1, +0.2/−0.1, H7) or, if there is nothing, in the general tolerance note of the title block.
  3. Work out the two limits.Add the upper deviation for the largest size and the lower deviation for the smallest. The Upper and lower limits box on the Limits and fits tab does this for you.

A simple routine for your own part

  1. Mark what touches what.On a print or a sketch, circle every face, hole and pin that touches, slides on or seals against another part. Only these need a tight tolerance.
  2. Pick a fit for every hole and shaft pair.Decide: must it slide, locate accurately, or stay pressed together? Use the Limits and fits tab to get the classes and numbers.
  3. Check every gap that matters with a stack-up.For example a cover over a shaft, or a part that must not hit another. Use the worked example on the stack-up tab as a pattern.
  4. Let the general tolerance cover the rest.Write ISO 2768-mK (or your company's note) in the title block. Use the charts to check the result is loose enough to be cheap and tight enough to work.
  5. Add GD&T where position or shape matters.Bolt hole patterns, bearing seats, sealing faces. Use the frame builder and let it check the rules.
  6. Check the cost.Open DFM review and look at the should-cost estimate. If tight tolerances drive the price, relax the ones that are not at an interface.
